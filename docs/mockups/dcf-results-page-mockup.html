The discount rate (WACC), explained

In plain words: a DCF adds up the cash a company is expected to earn in the future, then shrinks it back into today's money. A dollar later is worth less than a dollar now, because you could invest it elsewhere and because the future is uncertain. The discount rate is how hard it shrinks. Analysts call it WACC (weighted average cost of capital): the return that the company's investors and lenders, mixed together, demand for the risk they take.

Typical ranges are only a rule of thumb: large, stable companies often land around 7 to 9%, smaller or riskier ones around 10 to 12% or more.
